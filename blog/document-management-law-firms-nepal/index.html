Document Management

Document management for law firms in Nepal

Pleadings, court orders, correspondence and drafts — a firm's documents are its work product. Keeping them attached to the right matter is less about discipline than about where the files are allowed to live.

By the Tarikh team · Published July 15, 2026 · Updated October 16, 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR

File documents inside the matter they belong to, not in a folder tree that mirrors it. Use a naming convention that includes the date and the document type, so the newest version is obvious. Keep versions rather than overwriting, restrict access to the team on the matter, and make sure the file is still findable five years later by someone who has never worked on it.

Legal document management failures are rarely dramatic. Nobody loses a pleading entirely. What actually happens is subtler and more expensive: the wrong version gets printed before a hearing, a court order sits in an email inbox nobody else can see, or a partner spends forty minutes looking for an agreement they know exists.

None of that is a discipline problem. It is a consequence of where the files were allowed to live.

Start with one rule: the document belongs to the matter

Most firms organise files by client, or by year, or by document type, or by some combination that made sense when the firm was three people. The structure is not wrong, but it misses the thing people actually search with.

When someone in a law firm looks for a document, they almost always know the matter first. "Where is the written statement in the Sharma matter?" is the real query. Folder trees organised by client then year then document type require the person to reconstruct the tree before they can look, and every reconstruction is a chance to get it wrong.

The fix is to stop imitating a structure and start using one: file every document against the matter. Drafts, filed copies, court orders, correspondence, engagement letters, fee notes. The folder tree then becomes a view that the system generates, rather than a structure people have to maintain by hand.

Naming, briefly, and then stop thinking about it

If documents are attached to matters, filenames do much less work — but they still matter, mostly for the moment when three versions of the same document appear next to each other. A convention that survives contact with a busy firm is short:

  • Date first, in a sortable format: 2026-10-16 rather than 16-10-26. Sorting by name then sorts by date.
  • Document type next: Written Statement, Court Order, Engagement Letter.
  • Version or status last, and only if needed: v2, filed, final.

So: 2026-10-16 Written Statement v2.docx. Ten seconds to write, and it answers most of the questions anyone will have about the file without opening it.

Resist adding the client name, the court and the case number to filenames. Those are already on the matter. A filename that repeats everything the system already knows becomes too long to use and gets abbreviated inconsistently — which is how you end up with three files that are the same document.

Versions: keep them, do not overwrite them

Overwriting is the single most common source of document trouble in firms. It feels tidy, and it destroys the evidence of what changed — which matters both when a junior needs to see what a partner amended and when a client asks what was filed on which date.

Two habits are enough:

  • Never overwrite a document that has left the firm — filed, served or sent to a client. If it changes, it becomes a new version, and the old one stays.
  • Keep filed copies separate from working drafts. The version you actually filed is the one that matters in a dispute, and it should not be the version someone is still editing.

Access: the team on the matter, and nobody else

Legal files are confidential by nature, and access should default to the people working on the matter rather than to the whole firm. That is not distrust — it is the structure that makes confidentiality a property of the system instead of a promise people have to keep.

In practice this means matters should be restrictable to an assigned team, and access changes should be recorded so that when someone leaves, you can see what they could see. The feature worth asking about in any system is not "can I set permissions" but "can I see the history of who had access, and when".

What to keep, and for how long, is a separate question. Firm policies differ, and so do record-keeping requirements and any rules that apply to a particular practice area. Confirm yours with the firm's own policy and the requirements that apply to you rather than relying on a default in a tool.

Make it findable by someone who was not there

The real test of a document system is a handover. Someone leaves, goes on long leave, or simply moves their attention to another matter. Can the next person find everything they need?

Three questions to test with:

  • Can I see every document in a matter in date order, with the filed copies marked?
  • Can I search across the firm for a document by its content, not just its name?
  • If a client asks what we filed and when, can I answer without emailing anyone?

If all three answers are yes, the system is doing its job. If the answer to any of them is "ask the person who handled it", the documents are attached to a person rather than to the matter.
