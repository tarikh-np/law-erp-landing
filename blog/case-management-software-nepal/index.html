Case management software for law firms in Nepal: what to look for

Moving a firm's case files into software is a long-term decision. These are the questions that separate a system your firm will still be using in five years from one that quietly gets abandoned in three months.

By the Tarikh team · Published August 5, 2026 · Updated October 16, 2026 · 10 min read

TL;DR

Judge a system on whether the matter is the centre of everything — dates, documents, parties and billing all attached to it — and on whether it understands Nepali court dates and Bikram Samvat without a workaround. Then check the boring things: who can see what, what the audit trail shows, whether you can export your own data, and who supports you when something breaks. Ignore feature counts and screenshots.

Every law firm in Nepal now has some version of a case register. It might be a paper diary, a spreadsheet with a tab per advocate, or a shared drive of folders named after clients. Each of those works until it does not — and the moment it stops working is usually the moment a firm starts looking at case management software.

The problem is that evaluating software is much easier to do badly than well. Demos all look good. Feature lists all look long. What actually determines whether a system survives contact with a busy practice is whether its data model matches how a legal matter works, and whether it fits Nepali practice rather than requiring you to translate your work into someone else's assumptions.

Start from your own workflow, not the feature list

Before looking at any product, write down what happens in your firm between a client walking in and a matter closing. Most practices produce a list that looks something like this:

  • Intake: who the client is, who the opposing party is, what the dispute is about.
  • Filing: which court, what kind of case, what reference number.
  • Running the matter: hearings, Peshi dates, filings, correspondence, internal notes.
  • Documents: what was drafted, what was filed, what came back from the court.
  • Money: what was agreed, what has been billed, what is outstanding.
  • Closing: how the file is archived and what is kept.

Anything a system cannot express on that list is a gap you will end up covering with a spreadsheet — and a system plus a spreadsheet is worse than one or the other, because now there are two sources of truth again.

The non-negotiables

1. The matter is the centre of the data model

Dates, documents, parties, notes and invoices should all hang off a matter, and a client should be able to hold several matters. If the system is organised around clients, or around tasks, you will spend the next two years re-parenting records.

2. Court-aware dates, including Bikram Samvat

This is where generic international legal software usually falls over. A system built for another jurisdiction will accept a date, but it will not understand a Peshi or Tarikh date, will not store BS and AD side by side, and will not give the firm one view of what is coming up across every court. Ask directly: how does a hearing get recorded, and what does the firm-wide calendar show?

3. Documents attached to the matter, with versions

Documents living in a matter are documents somebody will actually find. Documents living in a shared drive with a naming convention are documents that survive only as long as the convention does.

4. Billing in NPR, connected to the matter

Time and expenses recorded against the matter, an invoice that references the matter, and a view of what is outstanding. If billing is a separate tool, the two will drift.

5. Roles and an audit trail

Partners, associates, paralegals and administrative staff should not all see the same things. And an audit log — who changed what, when — is the feature firms appreciate most the first time a client disputes a date.

6. Your data, exportable

Ask how you would get your data out. A vendor that cannot answer this clearly is telling you something. Your matters are your firm's records, and the ability to export them should not depend on a support request going well.

7. Support you can actually reach

Software fails at inconvenient times, and Nepali firms work to court calendars, not to a vendor's ticket queue. Ask who supports you, in what hours, and in which language.

Questions worth asking on the call

These tend to produce more useful answers than any feature walkthrough:

  • Show me recording a hearing for a matter that is already at High Court level, then show me the firm-wide calendar.
  • What happens to the old date when a hearing is adjourned?
  • Can I restrict a matter so only the assigned team sees it?
  • How do I produce a list of everything outstanding for a client?
  • What does the audit trail look like for a single matter?
  • How long does migration take for a firm with a few hundred open matters, and who does it?
  • What happens if we decide to leave?

What to ignore

Three things reliably mislead firms during evaluation.

Feature counts. A list of sixty features tells you nothing about whether the five you need are joined up. Three connected features beat twenty disconnected ones.

Polished screenshots. A screenshot is a design mock. Ask to see a real account with real-looking data — including the ugly parts, like a matter that has been closed and reopened.

"AI" as a headline. There are genuinely useful things automation can do with legal records. There are also a lot of products where the word is doing more work than the feature. If a claim matters to you, ask to see it operating on your own sample data.

How to run a fair trial

A trial that runs on one enthusiastic advocate's laptop will always succeed and always mislead. Better:

  • Enter ten real, closed matters — including one that was messy.
  • Have two people use it for three weeks, one of them sceptical.
  • Run one week of real court dates through it alongside your existing register.
  • Generate one real invoice from it.
  • At the end, ask the sceptic what they would miss.

That last question is the one that predicts adoption.
