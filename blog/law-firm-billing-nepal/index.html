Law firm billing in Nepal: getting paid without the awkwardness

Most billing problems in a law firm are not client problems. They are timing problems and record problems. Here is how to fix both before they turn into a collection conversation.

By the Tarikh team · Published August 15, 2026 · Updated October 16, 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR

Agree the fee basis and write it down on the matter before work starts. Record time as it happens, not from memory at month end. Invoice on a fixed schedule rather than when the matter ends. Make the invoice unambiguous — matter reference, period, currency in NPR, what was done. Then follow up on a schedule you decided in advance, so the follow-up is a process rather than a confrontation.

Ask a group of Nepali advocates what they dislike most about running a practice and billing rarely comes first, but it comes up quickly. The discomfort is usually not about money. It is about the moment when a firm has to ask a client for payment on work whose value was never stated clearly at the beginning.

Good billing practice is mostly about removing that moment. Not by becoming assertive, but by making the fee, the record and the rhythm unambiguous from day one.

1. Agree the fee basis, and put it on the matter

There are several common ways a Nepali firm charges — a fixed fee for a defined piece of work, an hourly or per-appearance rate, a monthly retainer, or a combination. Which one you use is a commercial decision for the firm and its client, and nothing here changes that.

What matters is that the basis is recorded on the matter and visible to whoever raises the invoice six weeks later. When the fee basis lives only in the intake conversation, the invoice is reconstructed from memory, and reconstructed invoices are the ones clients query.

Write down, at intake:

  • What the fee is for — the scope, in the client's words as well as yours.
  • What is not included. This single line prevents more disputes than any other.
  • What is charged separately: court fees, travel, printing, expert costs, filing charges.
  • How often you will invoice.

2. Record time as it happens

Reconstructing a month of work from memory at month end always produces the same three failures: it under-counts, it loses the detail that justifies the charge, and it takes a partner an entire day that could have gone to clients.

The alternative is unglamorous. Record entries against the matter as the work happens — a hearing attended, a filing drafted, a call with the client, a document reviewed. One line: date, matter, what was done, how long. In Tarikh that record sits on the matter itself, so it does not need to be re-entered anywhere else.

Two habits make this realistic at a busy firm:

  • Attach the entry to the event that caused it. If the entry hangs off the hearing or the filing, the work item and the billable item are the same object.
  • Record expenses at the moment they happen. Court fees and travel are the entries that get forgotten, and they are also the ones clients accept most readily when documented.

3. Invoice on a schedule, not at the end

The single biggest change most firms can make is to stop treating the invoice as something that happens when a matter finishes. Long matters then produce a single enormous bill that the client has had a year to stop expecting — and which arrives at the worst possible moment for both sides.

Monthly invoicing, or at an agreed milestone, does three things at once: it keeps the amounts small enough to be paid without a discussion, it keeps the client in contact with what is being done, and it means the firm's own cash flow is no longer hostage to one matter's timeline.

Set the schedule at intake and tell the client. "We invoice monthly" is a much easier sentence than "we need to talk about the balance".

4. Make the invoice unambiguous

An invoice that cannot be understood at a glance will be paid late, and the delay will usually be blamed on the client's cash flow when it is really a document problem. The minimum a legal invoice should carry:

  • The matter and case reference, so the client can match it to their file.
  • The period covered — a range, not just an issue date.
  • A short narrative for each entry: what was done, and when.
  • The amount, in NPR, with the currency stated rather than implied.
  • The total outstanding, including any earlier unpaid invoice.
  • How to pay, and by when.

Where the firm is registered for tax, the invoice also has to carry whatever particulars the applicable rules require. Confirm those with your accountant — they change, and getting them wrong is a filing problem, not a client-communication problem.

5. Track what is outstanding, per client and per matter

Most firms know their total receivables. Far fewer can answer "what is outstanding from this client, across all their matters, right now" without going through files. That is the number that matters when a client asks for an update, and it is the number that reveals a slow payer before the balance becomes uncomfortable.

A simple ageing view is enough: current, 30 days, 60 days, 90+. Once you can see the shape of it, the conversation changes from "please pay" to "the March invoice is now at 60 days — can you confirm when it will be settled?" The second sentence is much easier to send, and it is far more effective.

6. Put the follow-up on a schedule too

Decide the follow-up cadence before you need it. For example: a courtesy note at 15 days past due, a call at 30, a written summary of the account at 60, and a decision at 90 about whether the matter continues on the same terms.

The point of writing it down in advance is that it stops being a judgement call made by whoever is having a difficult week. The follow-up becomes a step in a process, which is a much easier thing to perform than a confrontation.

What a firm should be able to answer

As a quick check on how well billing is wired into your practice, see whether you can answer these without opening a file:

  • What is the fee basis on this matter?
  • What have we billed this client so far?
  • What is outstanding, and how old is it?
  • What is recorded but not yet billed?
  • Which matters have had work done but no invoice raised this month?

If the answers live in three different places, the problem is not discipline — it is that the records are separate from the work. Connecting them is the point of putting billing inside the same system as the matters.
