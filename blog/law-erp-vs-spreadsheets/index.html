Law ERP vs spreadsheets for Nepali law firms

Spreadsheets are not the problem. They are a genuinely good general-purpose tool for a small firm. The problem is the moment they stop being the right tool — and firms usually notice a year after it happened.

By the Tarikh team · Published July 25, 2026 · Updated October 16, 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR

Spreadsheets handle lists well and relationships badly. A law firm's data is mostly relationships: a client has matters, a matter has hearings, documents, parties and invoices. Once a firm is tracking those across several tabs and files, the spreadsheet is no longer saving time — it is moving the work to a different place, where it is harder to see. A law ERP puts the relationships in the data model instead of in someone's head.

Every firm that has moved to practice management software started on spreadsheets, and very few of them regret starting there. Spreadsheets are free, flexible, and immediately understood by everyone. They scale from a solo advocate to a firm of five without any decision-making at all.

Then, quietly, something changes. And it is worth being precise about what, because "spreadsheets are messy" is not a diagnosis, and it does not tell you when to switch.

What spreadsheets are genuinely good at

It is worth saying plainly, because the argument for software is often made badly: a spreadsheet is excellent at lists. A list of open matters, a list of clients and phone numbers, a list of expenses, a monthly fee calculation. It is fast, it is transparent, and anyone can fix it without a support ticket.

If your firm's problem is "we need one list and we need it today", a spreadsheet is the right answer, and it will stay the right answer for longer than software vendors like to admit.

Where it stops working

The shift happens when your information stops being a list and starts being a set of relationships. Five symptoms, in roughly the order firms notice them.

1. The same fact lives in more than one tab

A client's phone number appears in the client tab, in the matter tab, and in the billing sheet — and one of them is wrong. Nobody knows which one is authoritative, because a spreadsheet has no concept of a fact having a home. It only has cells.

2. A hearing date needs a vlookup to be useful

A date in isolation is not useful. A date next to its matter, court and case reference is. In a spreadsheet, joining those two things depends on someone matching rows correctly every time — which is exactly the kind of task that is fine when you are fresh and dangerous when you are busy.

3. Nobody can say who changed what

Spreadsheets do not have permissions in any useful sense and they do not have an audit trail. If a date changed, or a fee was edited, the file will not tell you when or by whom. In a firm where billing disputes and hearsay dates matter, that is a real gap.

4. One file is one point of failure

Case registers live in one workbook, often on one machine, sometimes emailed around as "register-final-v3". Two people editing a copy is a merge conflict that nobody resolves, and a lost laptop is a lost register.

5. Firm-wide questions require a manual exercise

Questions like "what is outstanding from clients this quarter", "which matters have had no activity in 60 days", or "what does next week look like across all courts" all need someone to assemble an answer by hand. Those questions get asked more as a firm grows, and each one consumes an hour of a fee-earner's time.

What "law ERP" actually means

The term ERP comes from manufacturing and finance, where it describes one system holding connected records instead of a dozen disconnected ones. A law ERP applies the same idea to a legal practice: one place where the relationships between clients, matters, hearings, documents, parties and money are expressed as data rather than as separate lists.

Concretely, that produces a handful of things a spreadsheet cannot give you:

  • One authoritative record per fact. The client's number is stored once. Every view of that client uses it.
  • Dates that know what they belong to. A hearing is attached to a matter, so the firm-wide calendar is a view of existing data rather than a report somebody builds.
  • Access control that means something. A matter can be limited to the team on it.
  • An audit trail. Changes are attributed and timestamped.
  • Billing connected to the work. Time and expenses recorded against the matter produce an invoice that references it, so the money question and the case question are answered from the same records.
  • A backup that is not somebody's laptop.

What it does not give you is flexibility. A spreadsheet will do anything you ask, immediately, including the wrong thing. Software imposes a structure, and imposing structure is the point.

A migration path that does not hurt

Firms delay the move because they imagine migrating everything at once. They do not have to.

  1. Start with new matters only. Enter every matter opened from next Monday into the new system, and leave closed and dormant files where they are until they come up again.
  2. Bring the live court dates across first. If you migrate nothing else this month, migrate the dates. That is where the risk sits. The Peshi and Tarikh date tracking guide covers the process for running the two in parallel for a short period.
  3. Keep the old register read-only. Stop editing it. A register that is still being updated is still a competing source of truth.
  4. Move billing once dates are stable. Billing depends on matters being in place, so it is the last step, not the first.
  5. Delete the duplicated spreadsheet. This is the step firms skip, and it is the step that decides whether the migration holds.

When to stay on spreadsheets

There are honest cases for waiting. If you are a solo practitioner with a handful of active matters and one assistant, a well-kept register plus a calendar may genuinely be enough, and a system introduces overhead you do not need. The signal to move is not firm size — it is whether you have started answering firm-wide questions by hand, or whether more than one person needs to see the same date without asking.
