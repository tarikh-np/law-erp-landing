Peshi / Tarikh Dates

How to track Peshi and Tarikh dates without the confusion

Court dates are the one piece of information a Nepali law firm cannot afford to lose. Here is a process for recording, checking and handing over Peshi and Tarikh dates that survives a busy week.

By the Tarikh team · Published August 25, 2026 · Updated October 16, 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR

Keep one record of every court date, not several. Record each Peshi or Tarikh date against the matter it belongs to, store both the Bikram Samvat and the AD date, name one person who owns each date, and check the coming two weeks out loud at a fixed time each week. Almost every missed date we hear about is a duplicate-record problem or a handover problem, not an arithmetic problem.

In a Nepali law firm, the Peshi date is the rhythm the whole week is built around. Partners plan around it, juniors prepare for it, and clients call about it. And yet, in a lot of firms, the authoritative copy of that date lives in a paper diary that one person holds, with a second copy in a spreadsheet that nobody has updated since the last time it was printed.

This guide is about fixing that, in a way that does not depend on anyone becoming more careful. If the process relies on people remembering, it will fail in the week you can least afford it.

What Peshi and Tarikh actually refer to

The two words are used together so often that firms sometimes use them as synonyms, and sometimes distinguish them. In day-to-day practice:

  • Tarikh is the date fixed for a matter — "the date of the case". It is the entry every firm writes down.
  • Peshi is the listing itself: the appearance of the matter in court on that date.

How much a firm distinguishes the two varies. What matters for record-keeping is that you pick one convention, state it in your own template, and use it consistently — because the failure mode is not the wording, it is two people recording the same event under two different labels and then disagreeing about whether the date is booked.

The five ways court dates actually get lost

Most missed dates trace back to one of a small number of causes. It helps to know which one you have, because the fix is different for each.

1. Two records, no owner

A diary and a spreadsheet both claim to be authoritative. When they disagree, nobody is sure which is right, and the disagreement is discovered on the morning of the hearing.

2. The date is stored without the matter

A date in a personal notebook that says "Bagmati, 12th" is not usable the following week. A date is only useful next to the matter, the court and the case reference it belongs to.

3. Only one calendar is stored

Court communication frequently runs in Bikram Samvat, while a firm's internal reporting, salaries and client emails run in AD. If only one is written down, someone converts it later from memory, usually under time pressure.

4. The change is never propagated

A date gets adjourned. The person who attended updates their own copy. The client, the billing record and the partner's diary still show the old date, and the firm's next deadline is now wrong in three places.

5. The handover is verbal

Someone goes on leave, and the handover is "the diary is on my desk". A single verbal handover is the most common cause of a missed date we see in firms of every size.

The dual-calendar problem, with an example

Bikram Samvat and the Gregorian calendar do not line up neatly. A BS year begins on 1 Baisakh, which falls in mid-April — for example, 1 Baisakh 2083 corresponds to 14 April 2026. The BS year count sits roughly 56 years and 8 months ahead, which is why a date in early 2026 can still be in BS 2082.

Now add the part that catches people out: BS month lengths vary from year to year. There is no fixed arithmetic that converts a BS date to an AD date correctly for every month, which is why every conversion should come from an up-to-date Nepali patro rather than from a rule of thumb.

A worked example. Today — 16 October 2026 — is Ashwin 30, 2083 in Bikram Samvat. A hearing recorded as "Kartik 15" with no year is therefore ambiguous to anyone reading it in November: is that the Kartik that has just passed or the one approaching? The fix is small and worth insisting on:

  • Write the BS date with its year and month: 15 Kartik 2083.
  • Write the AD date beside it: 1 November 2026.
  • Never store one without the other.

That single rule removes an entire category of "I thought that was next month" conversations.

A process that holds up

Five rules. None of them require software, though a practice management system makes the first four automatic.

Rule 1: one register

Choose one place where a court date becomes official. Everything else — the partner's diary, the client email, the WhatsApp message — is a copy of that register, never an alternative to it. If two systems both claim to be authoritative, one of them has to be demoted, and it is quicker to decide this now than on the morning of a hearing.

Rule 2: a date without a matter is not recorded

Every date carries the matter, the court and the case reference. A date you cannot attach to a case is a date you cannot act on.

Rule 3: both calendars, always

BS and AD recorded together, at the moment the date is received. No later conversion step, because the later conversion step is the one that gets skipped.

Rule 4: one named owner per date

For each upcoming date, one person is responsible for it. Not a team, not a role title — a name. When a date changes, that person updates the register, and the register is what triggers the client update and the diary change.

Rule 5: a fixed weekly read-through

Pick a time — Monday morning works for most firms — and read out the next two weeks of dates matter by matter. Fifteen minutes. The value is not the reading; it is that a date with no preparation attached becomes visible while there is still time to do something about it.

When a date changes

Adjournments are the point at which a well-run register and a badly-run one diverge. The sequence that works is: update the register first, then tell the client, then update anything that depends on the date — preparation tasks, billing entries, the team calendar. Doing it in that order means the register is never the stale copy.

One habit worth adding: record the old date with a note that it was vacated, rather than overwriting it. A firm that keeps the history can answer "why did this matter sit idle for six weeks" without relying on anyone's recollection.

Where a system helps, and where it does not

Software does not fix a process that has no owner. What it does fix is the duplication problem: if there is one register, court hearing tracking can attach every date to its matter, keep the firm-wide calendar in one view, and give the partner's phone the same answer as the office desktop. In Tarikh, dates are recorded against the matter, the upcoming-hearings view spans the whole firm, and support for the Bikram Samvat calendar sits alongside AD dates so nobody has to hold two calendars in their head.
