Nepal Court System

Nepal court hierarchy explained: Supreme Court, High Courts and District Courts

How Nepal's courts are organised from the Supreme Court down to District Courts, and why the level a case sits at changes how a firm has to track it.

By the Tarikh team · Published September 1, 2026 · Updated October 16, 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

Nepal's judiciary runs on three main tiers — the Supreme Court (सर्वोच्च अदालत), a High Court (उच्च अदालत) in each province, and a District Court (जिल्ला अदालत) in each district. Cases normally start in a District Court and move up on appeal. For a firm, the court level determines which hearing types, case-number formats and date conventions you have to record, which is why the level belongs on the matter record itself rather than in someone's memory.

Nepal's court system is organised into a clear hierarchy, and every firm working in Nepali courts has to work with it. Whether a matter is sitting in a District Court in Kathmandu or on appeal before the Supreme Court, the level it sits at shapes the procedural steps, the paperwork and the hearing schedule.

The three main tiers

Nepal's judiciary operates on three main tiers: the Supreme Court at the apex, a High Court at provincial level, and District Courts at district level. Each tier has its own jurisdiction and handles a different stage of a case.

1. Supreme Court (सर्वोच्च अदालत)

The Supreme Court is Nepal's highest court and sits in Kathmandu. It hears appeals from the High Courts, exercises writ jurisdiction, and interprets the Constitution. For most firms, Supreme Court work is a smaller, higher-stakes slice of the practice: constitutional questions, major appeals and matters that set precedent.

2. High Courts (उच्च अदालत)

Nepal has seven provinces, and each has a High Court. High Courts hear appeals from the District Courts and also take original jurisdiction in certain matters. They sit in the middle of the appellate chain, so a case can pass through a High Court before it can reach the Supreme Court at all.

3. District Courts (जिल्ला अदालत)

District Courts are the courts of first instance for most matters. There is one in each district, and they handle the bulk of civil and criminal work: property and land disputes, family matters, contract disputes and much of the day-to-day litigation a firm runs. For most Nepali firms, this is where the majority of Peshi dates are set.

Specialised courts, tribunals and the "one more level" question

Beyond the three main tiers, Nepal's system also includes courts and tribunals set up for particular categories of cases. The exact list, and which matters fall under each, is set by law and can change.

This is worth being careful about, because it is one of the places where a firm's own spreadsheet template quietly goes stale. A tribunal created for one category of dispute changes where a case has to be filed and which deadlines apply. Treat the current list as something to confirm against the Nepal Law Commission or the court concerned rather than something to infer from an old matter file.

Why the court level matters for case management

For a law firm, the court level is not a label — it changes the data you have to keep. Different levels differ in:

  • Hearing types and stages. A first hearing in a District Court and an appellate hearing are not the same event, even though both produce a Peshi date.
  • Case number and filing formats. The reference a firm uses to identify a matter is built differently at each level.
  • Date conventions. Court dates are commonly communicated in Bikram Samvat, while a firm's internal reporting often runs in AD. Anything that stores only one of the two creates avoidable checking.
  • Documents. What has to be filed, and by whom, differs by level and by matter stage.

When a matter sits across more than one court — a District Court case with a High Court appeal — the firm needs the level recorded on the matter itself, not held in a partner's memory or in a spreadsheet column somebody renamed two years ago.

What to record about the court on a matter

If the court level is going to be useful, it has to be recorded consistently on every matter. In practice a firm needs six things, and a matter is hard to work on when any of them is missing:

  • The court, by name and level. "District Court" on its own is ambiguous — there is one in each district, so the district belongs in the field too.
  • The case reference as the court writes it. Copy it exactly rather than normalising it. A reference that has been tidied up by hand is harder to match against anything the court issues.
  • The parties, as listed. The order the court uses matters when you are reading an order or a cause list quickly.
  • The filing date, because it is the anchor for everything that follows and the first thing anyone asks about later.
  • The next date, in both calendars. Tarikh or Peshi, with the Bikram Samvat and AD dates stored together so nobody converts under pressure.
  • Who from the firm is attending, so preparation and attendance have a named owner.

None of this is exotic. The point is that all six should live on the matter rather than being split between a case register, a diary and one advocate's notebook — because the moment they are split, the level stops being reliable information and becomes a field somebody keeps forgetting to fill in.

Working across courts without losing the thread

The harder case is a matter that moves. A District Court case that goes up on appeal picks up a second reference, a different court and a separate set of dates, while the original matter continues to exist. Firms handle this in one of two ways, and both are workable as long as the firm picks one and applies it everywhere:

  • One matter, multiple court records. The matter stays the single thread for the client and the fee, and each court level is recorded inside it. This is usually the better fit for a firm that bills a client once for the whole dispute.
  • A parent matter with a linked appeal record. Useful when the appeal is substantial enough to be worked by different people or billed separately — but only if the link between the two is explicit. Implicit links, which is what a shared filename amounts to, do not survive a handover.

Whichever you choose, the test is the same question a client asks: "where does my case stand right now?" If answering it means opening two registers and reconciling them by hand, the structure is costing the firm time on every status call — and status calls are exactly when a firm should look most organised.
